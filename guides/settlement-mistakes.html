Five mistakes

How a settlement goes wrong
← Back to the ledger

Guides · Settling up

A settlement that goes wrong rarely goes wrong dramatically. It is almost always one of five small errors, each of which is easy to catch if you know to look for it.

1. Adding buy-ins as a running total

Someone's record says $60. Was that three buy-ins of $20, or a $20 and a $40 top-up? Nobody remembers. And because the figure cannot be broken back down, it cannot be checked against anything — not against the tray, not against what anyone recalls.

The catch: keep every buy-in as a separate entry. A record that reads “20, 20, 20” can be audited. A record that reads “60” can only be believed.

2. Settling before everyone is counted

Two players are still counting, and someone starts paying out to the players who are already done. Then the last stack comes in and the total does not reconcile — but money has already moved, so now you are unwinding payments as well as hunting an error.

The catch: nobody pays anybody until every cash-out figure is recorded and the total has been checked against total buy-ins. It costs ninety seconds.

3. Netting through the pot instead of between players

A home game has no house, so there is no pot to pay into. But people reach for that model anyway: everyone hands cash to the banker, the banker pays the winners. It technically works, and it produces roughly twice as many transactions as necessary, each one an opportunity to make a mistake.

The catch: settle player-to-player. Match the biggest winner with the biggest loser and work down. A table of n players never needs more than n − 1 payments, and usually needs fewer.

4. Rounding at the wrong moment

This one is quiet and it is the most common source of a settlement being off by a small amount that nobody can locate. If you round each player's net to the nearest dollar and then settle, the rounded nets no longer sum to zero, and the difference has to land on somebody arbitrarily.

The catch: do all arithmetic in the smallest unit — cents, or won — and round only the final payment figures, if at all. Poker Ledger works in integer minor units throughout for this reason, and shows no decimal places for won and yen because those currencies do not have them.

5. Making the numbers fit

The count is $15 short. Someone says “I might have miscounted, call mine $15 more” and the books balance. Everyone is relieved and the evening ends.

This is the worst one, because it looks like a solution. What has actually happened is that one player absorbed the entire discrepancy, alone, without anyone deciding that was fair — and the real error is still there, uncorrected, ready to recur next week.

The catch: when the count is short, say so and look for the cause first. Check for an unrecorded rebuy, then a player who cashed out early, then the floor. Only when you have genuinely looked should you decide, deliberately and out loud, who absorbs it.

The check that catches all five

Before anyone pays anyone, total the buy-ins and total the cash-outs. They should be identical. That single comparison catches every error above, and it takes about fifteen seconds.

If they do not match, the direction tells you what to look for: more chips than buy-ins usually means a missed rebuy; fewer chips than buy-ins usually means a miscounted or missing stack. How it works covers what the calculator does once you have looked and the gap is still there.
